The 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria were developed jointly by the
American College of Rheumatology and the European League Against
Rheumatism to identify rheumatoid arthritis earlier in its course than
older criteria could, since early treatment leads to better outcomes.

The criteria combine four categories of information into a single score
out of ten. A patient reaching a score of six or more, and who has at
least one joint with confirmed synovitis that is not better explained by
another disease, is classified as having rheumatoid arthritis for
research and early-treatment purposes.

The four categories are:

1. Joint involvement: how many joints show swelling or tenderness, and
   whether they are small joints (such as the fingers, wrists, and toes)
   or large joints (such as shoulders, elbows, hips, and knees).
   Involvement of more small joints contributes more points than the
   same number of large joints, because small-joint symmetric
   involvement is more characteristic of RA.

2. Serology: whether blood tests for rheumatoid factor (RF) or
   anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (anti-CCP) are negative, weakly
   positive, or strongly positive. Strong positivity contributes more
   points than weak positivity, and both contribute more than a negative
   result.

3. Acute-phase reactants: whether inflammatory markers such as C-reactive
   protein (CRP) or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are abnormal.

4. Duration of symptoms: whether joint symptoms have been present for six
   weeks or longer, since RA is a chronic condition and short-lived joint
   symptoms are more often caused by other, self-limiting conditions.

It is important to note that this classification score is intended as a
research and early-identification tool to support clinical decision
making. It is not a substitute for a full clinical evaluation by a
rheumatologist, who will also consider imaging, other lab work, and the
exclusion of conditions that can mimic RA, such as psoriatic arthritis,
lupus, or gout.
