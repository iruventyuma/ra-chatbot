Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune condition in which the
immune system mistakenly attacks the synovium, the lining of the joints.
This produces inflammation that can thicken the synovium and eventually
damage the cartilage and bone inside the joint. RA most commonly affects
the small joints of the hands and feet first, typically in a symmetric
pattern, meaning if one hand is affected the other usually is too.

Common early symptoms include joint pain, swelling, warmth, and stiffness
that is often worse in the morning and can last more than 30 minutes.
Fatigue, low-grade fever, and a general feeling of being unwell are also
common. As the disease progresses without treatment, joint erosion and
deformity can occur, along with reduced range of motion and grip strength.

RA is different from osteoarthritis, which is caused by mechanical wear
and tear rather than autoimmune inflammation. Osteoarthritis tends to
affect joints asymmetrically and is more common in older adults, while RA
can begin at any age and follows the symmetric small-joint pattern
described above.

Risk factors associated with RA include a family history of the disease,
smoking, being female, and certain genetic markers. Environmental factors
and infections are also being studied as possible triggers in people who
are genetically predisposed.

Early diagnosis and treatment are important because joint damage from RA
is often irreversible once it occurs. Disease-modifying antirheumatic
drugs (DMARDs), including biologic agents, are the primary treatment
approach and aim to slow or stop disease progression rather than only
relieving symptoms. Physical therapy, occupational therapy, and lifestyle
adjustments can also help patients maintain function and quality of life.
